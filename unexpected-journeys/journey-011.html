The Moons Over Bracken Hollow

A village no one can leave. A stone that chooses who is taken.

A list from Wyhan draws the Embers to Bracken Hollow, a village shut under three eclipsed moons that no one has been able to leave for a month. Sergeant Boyd explains the turn of the white, red and black moons, and the eclipse that takes one villager each time. The party climbs the hill with Corvin and Ked and brings down the stone monument by darkening the three rings on its chest, but the Emberwake's fury takes Aratos and Roshi has to drop him. Beneath the hill are three recesses, and three things to find in the village before the next eclipse.

The road out of Kalaman ran through country that had already begun to turn toward autumn. Along the way Kakashi called a halt of an hour or so, sat down with the charcoal and incense he had bought from Wyhan, and muttered over them with his sleeve drawn across his work. The smoke thickened into a paste, the paste into feathers, and a raven shook itself out of the air. It was the same bird he had lost, with the same wit and the same memory, and it took to the sky above the party as though it had never left.

He had a second piece of business from the apothecary. A note had caught up with them on the road, and the bird that carried it had already gone. It was a list in Wyhan's hand: willowbark from any stream, pine resin, graveyard moss, and frostlace, which was plainly the one that mattered. Kakashi held the note open as he walked and asked the others to keep their eyes sharp. Mayriel laid a hand on his shoulder, and for a moment his old, weathered eyes saw a little further than they should have. There was a line of trees ahead, and beyond it the sound of water.

The creek was small and the willows leaned into it. Kakashi knew at a glance where the best bark would be and cut it from the roots that trailed in the current. While he worked, Jasper and Mayriel looked downstream and saw the shapes of houses through the trunks, a gate, and a flag stirring on its staff just inside. The news was passed along in whispers, though nobody could afterward say why they had been whispering. There were no guards at the gate and no wall worth the name. It was a plain village of the same sort as Vogler, and whatever protection it had came from the forest that hid it. A small sign named the place: Bracken Hollow.

Roshi had heard of frostlace. It was a small, pale flower that came into bloom with the cold, and with autumn arriving it would be starting now if it grew here at all. Kakashi found a stand of pines on the way in, set his dagger to the bark, and told the others to go on while the resin ran. He would catch up.

The village opened onto a square with thatched houses around it, a few buildings of two or three floors, and a cemetery a short walk off. Past the rooftops rose a hill, and on the hill stood a tall block of stone, a monument of some kind, dark against the sky. Nothing moved. The doors were shut, and the place had the look of somewhere people had been keeping indoors for a good deal longer than a few days.

Then the light went. It was as though dusk had come hours early, the sun washed out behind a haze. When they looked up, the three moons were standing together in the sky, the white, the red and the black, each laid over the next in eclipse. Kakashi walked back the way he had come to see whether the gloom lay only over the village. It was the same ten paces on, and when he turned around, the forest behind them was dark as well.

Mayriel and Roshi had seen that sky before. It had hung over them a month ago, on the night an old shrine answered them and the gods spoke. An alignment like that came once in a year, and the year had not turned. Aratos knew it too. It was under that same eclipse that his vision of the coming war had found him, and now a pale haze seemed to drift down from the moons and settle in his stomach like the start of a sickness. Mayriel's longbow hummed its warning against her back, and a howl rose out of the trees.

They made ready. Mayriel spoke a prayer over those who would stand in front, and they felt themselves made sturdier by it. Roshi conjured an illusion in the road, a griffon, silent but restless on its feet, and stepped inside it to wait. Kakashi chose that moment to go to the graveyard. He needed moss, and he wanted bones, and he promised again to catch up. Being human, he could see nothing in that dark, so he lit a torch and carried it between the headstones while his companions stood with drawn weapons and watched the small flame move away from them.

The moss was easy. He found it on the headstones and pocketed a handful. The graves were another matter, because a great many of them were fresh. Kakashi knelt at one of the newest, set the torch down, and began to dig with his hands. He had cleared enough earth to see the curve of a skull when a hand closed on his shoulder and lifted him to his feet. The man behind it was old and dark-skinned, with grey hair and a soldier's armor gone to tatters. He said only that Kakashi needed to go, and quickly. The howling was much closer now.

The wolves came out of the dark at the others first. There were four of them, black as pitch and heavy in the coat, larger than any wolf had a right to be, with slaver hanging from their jaws and blood already on one muzzle. Jasper loosed before they had closed and put the nearest one down in the road. Mayriel spoke a blessing over her companions. Across the graveyard the old soldier saw what was happening, swore, asked Kakashi whether those were his people, and told him to stay hidden. He put two arrows into the night and both went wide.

Aratos covered the ground at a run, went down into a slide to make the last of the distance, and came up swinging the Emberwake. It felt right to have that axe in his hand. Orange and green fire whipped off the blade as it fell, and the wolf beneath it was opened from shoulder to flank, the wound seared shut as it was made. The green flame jumped from the dead animal to the next and scorched it. A third snapped at Aratos and found only air. The scorched one went for the griffon, was not fooled for long, and sprang at what was inside. Roshi had been waiting for exactly that with her sword levelled like a spit, and the wolf ran itself onto the blade. Jasper's second arrow took the last one through the neck as it gathered to leap.

The old soldier was already waving them toward the houses. They all needed to come inside, he said, because there would be more. He turned to collect Kakashi, found him gone, and feared for a moment that the wolves had taken him. Then he saw the torch back at the graveside and the wizard on his knees again. There was no time for this, the man told him, and did not bother to take him by the shoulder a second time. Kakashi came away with a set of bones in his bag. Overhead the raven watched other figures hurrying between the houses with sacks on their backs, and saw doors open just long enough to take them in.

The house they were brought to was dim and run down. A handful of villagers sat close together inside. The ones who had come in from the dark were out of breath, and the sacks they set on the floor held corn and other vegetables, enough to see that they had been out gathering food. When the door was shut the old soldier apologized for the abrupt introduction, and then for something larger. He was sorry, he said, that they had wandered into this town at all.

His name was Boyd, a sergeant once, long retired, who had made the Hollow his home. He would do what he could to look after them, though they seemed able to look after themselves, apart from the one who kept going back to the graveyard. They would be fed. All he asked was that they cause no trouble with the people here. They would be shut in for some hours until the wolves moved off. He asked for their names. They told him they were the Embers. Kakashi introduced himself as the famous Kakashi, with enough conviction that Boyd gave him a small look of respect. Jasper offered himself as the unfamous Jasper Fox, which had the advantage of being true.

Then Boyd gave them the long and short of it. Nobody had been able to leave Bracken Hollow for about a month. It had begun on the night of the eclipse. A person who walked out past the edge of the village walked back in on the far side of it. The moons had hung in eclipse ever since, but each of them took its turn in the sky, and each turn brought something with it.

The white moon was the longest, about half of what passed for a day, and the safest. Under it people felt a little sick, and echoes of the villagers went about the streets living an ordinary day as if nothing had changed. Some folk recognized the echoes. A few had watched their own. Boyd's advice was to pay those phantoms no mind. The red moon brought a battle into the town itself. The village militia had gone out to meet it the first time, and most of them had died there. The black moon, which was the one standing now, brought the wolves, and it made no difference how many were killed, because they always came back. It was also the only time the farms gave any food. So the people of the Hollow went out among the wolves to gather what they could, and what they carried home had to last through the rest. Boyd's advice was to stay off the streets under the red moon and the black.

After the three came the eclipse itself, which lasted about an hour. The party had seen the monument on the hill. They were not to go there. During the eclipse it woke and stood as a thing of living stone, and every eclipse, someone was taken.

As he spoke of it the party began to notice what the villagers had noticed long ago. A faint glow lay on everyone in the room. On the Embers it was white and red. On one man, sitting among the others, it was white and red and black. Boyd explained it as best he understood it. Everyone carried the white. Fewer carried the red. Only a handful carried the black, five or six at a time, and it was from that handful that one was chosen, at no one's bidding but the eclipse's. When it happened, nobody could say. The chosen simply vanished. Around two hundred people had lived in Bracken Hollow, and forty or so were gone. Some had died to wolves, some in the red moon's fighting, and most had been taken from the hill. The dead who could be buried had been buried with honor.

The Embers asked what they could think to ask. Nobody had tried to fight the monument. The militia were dead, and those left were farmers and working people. Boyd himself had been a soldier, though he admitted with a tired sort of humor that he had missed the day the army taught what to do about an eclipse golem. As for magic, the village had kept one person who understood such things, a healer and something of a mystic called Marigold. Her cottage stood just outside the Hollow, and she travelled between villages tending the sick. She had not been in the village on the night it closed. Frostlace he knew by name and no more. A farmer a few houses down, Gribb Brack, would be the one to ask, and Boyd offered to take them there under the white moon, when it was safer to walk the street.

Aratos pressed him on one point. What happened if someone the eclipse had not chosen went up the hill? Boyd said they had sent no one up there. He was picking his words with care, and while he did, a man at the back of the room rolled his eyes. He was one of those who had come in with the food, a broad-shouldered man in his forties with a rough beard. That was not the full truth, he said, now was it, Boyd. His name was Corvin. Boyd told him this was not the time. Corvin answered that they were stuck here regardless and the strangers might as well have all of it.

What Corvin told them was this. When someone was chosen, the village had a choice of its own. Another of the black-touched could be sent up the hill in that person's place, and the eclipse would take the one who was sent. Some skills, he said, were still needed to keep this town together. Kakashi asked who made those decisions. Corvin turned the question on Boyd: well, who did have to make them? Boyd, who had been calm until then, was angry now. Nobody should have to make that decision for another person. Lives were not to be picked out and thrown away because it was convenient. Corvin said only that if the option was there, they had to make the best of it. Kakashi looked from one to the other and asked whether anything else had been left out, since the Embers would most likely be going out there and putting their own lives on the line. Boyd said he was not asking them to fight the town's battles, and he would not stop them either. He was no dictator. He had only tried to hold the place together as best he could. If they went, he wished them luck.

Kakashi's thoughts went to the bones in his bag and to what he might make of them. His companions put a stop to it before he began. These people buried their dead with respect, and anyone who saw a neighbor's bones get up and walk would have every reason to turn on him. He let it lie for the moment.

Jasper, watching the room, saw that Corvin's eyes kept going to the man with the black in his glow. A little later Corvin spoke up again. People needed to eat. Nora was not well. Boyd agreed, took a share of the food in a sack, told the party to stay put, and slipped out into the dark. Kakashi sat down in a corner, closed his eyes, and watched through his raven. He saw Boyd move from cover to cover and wait while a single wolf padded through the square, and saw a door open on lamplight. Inside were women putting the food to a pot and people lying on cots at the back. The raven noticed one other thing. There were no birds in Bracken Hollow, and no animals of any kind except the wolves.

Jasper drifted over to Corvin and asked, quietly, whether there was anything he wanted to share about the man he kept looking at. Corvin told him it was nothing to worry about. But he had tensed at the question, and Jasper came away fairly sure of two things: that Corvin had wanted Boyd out of the house, and that he was working toward something.

Aratos could not sit still. He went to the door, looked out through its small window, and had it half open before Jasper stopped him to ask what the plan was. The plan, once they had one, was modest. They would go up to the hill during the eclipse, set themselves far enough off to watch, and decide in the moment whether to fight or to leave. None of them was content to wait indoors while someone vanished. Jasper suggested, a little too openly, that they bring help, meaning Corvin and the glowing man both, where he could keep an eye on them. Aratos took the idea at its plainest. He levelled his axe at the man and said, this one.

The man curled in on himself and said he did not want any trouble. The rest of the room stared. Jasper tried to explain that they only needed someone to help them find their way, and it went about as badly as it could have gone. It was Corvin who turned it. Come on, Ked, he said. These people were willing to try to help. They had to try something if they were ever going to get out of here. Aratos put a hand on Ked's shoulder, and gave him his word that no harm would come to him that the Embers could prevent. They would protect him with their lives. Ked looked at Corvin, and at the strangers, and slowly got to his feet. There were ten minutes left of the black moon.

They went through the village at a jog with a wolf pacing them along the edge of the dark. Partway up the hill they felt the hour change. That, they understood, was how the Hollow kept its time, by the turn in the air that could be felt on the skin. Above them the monument shuddered. Two arms unfolded from the block. The bulk of it rose and straightened, a head lifted from the shoulders, and on its chest three rings burned white, red and black.

Kakashi stopped on the slope, laid out the bones from his bag, and spoke over them. They rattled together and stood, and a small green light woke in the sockets of the skull as it turned toward him to wait for orders. Ked, who had not been warned, was terrified. As they came nearer the top, the glow on him grew brighter.

The stone thing stood where it was and did nothing. Aratos walked out ahead and called to it, asking what its intent was in this place. It gave no answer. But its attention came around to him, and a black glow kindled on Aratos where there had been only white and red. He had made himself one of those who could be taken, and the party thought they understood a little better how the choosing was done. Aratos looked back up at it. So it was the one that decided who was sacrificed, he said. It could not have him, or any of his companions. He had something else he needed to tend to.

He reached for a javelin. As he did, he felt the pull of the Emberwake, a strong urge to draw that and nothing else, and every other weapon sat wrong in his hand. Mayriel turned and asked Corvin how long the chosen had. He told her it came toward the end of the eclipse. They had most of an hour.

They spread out. Mayriel renewed her blessing. Jasper found a tree to shoot from, and from there he could also see Corvin standing close at Ked's back, a man making sure another man did not run. For a while nobody wanted to be the first to strike. In the end it was Kakashi who looked around for agreement and then threw lightning. It arced up the hill and fastened on the monument, a crackling tether between the wizard and the stone.

The thing straightened and looked at him. Ked began to back away, and backed into Corvin, and into the point of a dagger that had appeared in Corvin's hand. No, Corvin said. Ked stayed where he was.

Jasper's arrow flew out of the trees and struck home, but it bit less deeply than it should have, and he shouted to the others that ordinary weapons were doing half their work and they needed magic. Aratos hurled his javelin with the lightning of his ancestors in it, and the bolt ran harmlessly into the ground. Mayriel called down a soft dusk around herself that put fresh strength into anyone standing in it, and sent a weapon of pale light to strike the monument from behind. Then the monument leapt. It came down in front of Kakashi and brought its fists down after, again and again, and every blow missed him.

Aratos gave himself to his rage, and the spectral shapes of his ancestors gathered around the fight. The Emberwake went in deep. Roshi drew her moon-touched sword, and it hummed in the light of the white moon and flashed as she struck, divine fire bursting along the blade so that the stone reared back from it. Jasper threw his spear and saw it fall short. Kakashi kept the lightning running, put his own longsword into the skeleton's hands, and set it at the monument's back. Mayriel's guiding bolt left the whole creature glowing like a target. Behind them, Ked said he had to get out of there. Corvin told him he would stay and see it through, and Ked struck him.

Then the fight turned. The monument drove its legs into the hill. Something dark pulsed up out of the earth and into the stone, and rock closed over every wound they had given it. A wave of that same darkness rolled outward and withered everyone near. A fist came down on Aratos. The white ring flashed, and for a moment he felt his hold on the world around him slip. Corvin laid Ked out on the grass with a single blow, alive but senseless. And then the moons flashed overhead, and light fell on the hilltop like a hammer.

Roshi went down, kept from death only by the periapt she wore. Kakashi went down, and his lightning went out. Aratos was left standing, though barely. He put the Horn of Sir Cthondor to his lips and blew, and five spectral warriors answered it, striding out of the air with axes in their hands. He swallowed a healing draught and sent them in.

In the instant before the dark closed over him, Kakashi had seen something. The sky above Bracken Hollow was laid over with a lattice of spellwork, a net thrown across the whole village. Past it, for a heartbeat, he saw the true sun standing where the sun should be. And in the weave of the net there were three knots.

The warriors of the horn did what the living could not. One hauled Roshi clear of the monument's reach and took a blow across the back for it, and a potion was got down her throat. Another began dragging Kakashi clear. Others turned on a wolf that had followed them up the hill and cut it down, then went at the stone itself. On the grass, the glow on Ked flared. Roshi got to one knee, barely able to stand, reached across, and laid her hands on Kakashi. Then something broke through her usual calm. She cried out in plain frustration, and a divine light went out from her across the hilltop and closed wounds on every one of them.

Kakashi came awake shouting about the rings. They were to strike the rings, the three knots, because that was the only way the thing would fall. Jasper nocked one of the leeching arrows Old Man Jenkins had given him. The dried leech on its head stirred to life as it flew and bored into the stone. Kakashi got to his feet and sent his lightning straight into the black ring. It flared and then went dim, though the black glow on Aratos did not fade with it. Mayriel's next bolt struck the same ring and changed nothing more, and when her spectral weapon swung at the white ring it glanced away. The rings were not all equally easy to hit.

The darkness rolled out from the monument again, and the skeleton fell back into loose bones. This time, though, the hill gave the stone nothing, and its wounds stayed open. It swung at Aratos. Kakashi twisted the blow aside with a word, and it missed.

But the darkness had touched Aratos while the rage was on him, and that was enough. A fury that was not his own swelled in his chest. Where embers had been drifting from the axe there was now full flame along the whole length of the blade, and the sight of it was familiar. Corvin, who had never seen the monument fight like this, broke and ran.

The light fell on the hilltop a second time. Aratos, burning, hewed at the white ring, and it flared and went dark. One of the spectral warriors swung at the red and missed. A second struck it cleanly, and the last of the three rings dimmed.

The monument turned to swat at one of the warriors and left itself open. To Aratos, by then, everything on the hill had become fire, and this was the one great fire that had to be put out. He dragged the Emberwake through the earth and brought it up through the creature's neck. The stone ran like lava where the blade passed. The head came away, and the body fell.

A beam of light rose from the hilltop, red and white and black together. Aratos stood over the wreck, coughing with rage, still lost to it, with his friends the nearest living things in reach. He had almost no strength left to stand on. Roshi circled wide around him, stepping over Ked where he lay, came in from the side, and struck Aratos once, a blow meant to drop him and nothing more. It was enough. He dropped where he stood. The warriors of the horn went still and then closed in a ring about him, facing outward.

The hill was quiet after that. Kakashi went over the fallen stone and studied the rings. They were not the source of anything. They were receivers, taking in a working that flowed to them from somewhere else, and he knew from his own raven that a thing bound in that fashion can be called back again unless the binding itself is undone. The others, looking at the light, saw that it did not rise from the hilltop at all. It came from inside the hill.

Kakashi climbed to where the monument had stood and found a crevice. He went down with Jasper. One of the others waited partway up to listen for them, and the rest kept watch over Aratos. Below was a single closed cavern with three recesses cut into the stone. Kakashi understood it once he had seen it. This was the place the spell was bound to. Three rings, three recesses, three phases of the moons. Three things were needed here to break it, and those three things were somewhere in the village.

They had until the next eclipse, which was a little less than a day. Above them the hour of this one had not yet run out. Aratos lay senseless among his spectral guard, and Ked lay where Corvin had left him. Whether the eclipse would take anyone this time, none of them could yet say.
